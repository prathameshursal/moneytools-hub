EMI vs renting: which actually wins?

Updated 17 October 2026 · MoneyTools Hub Team · Information only, not financial advice

The internet gives two confident answers to this question and they contradict each other. Here is the honest version: both can win, and the numbers decide which one wins for you.

The real trade

Buying a home means paying an EMI that builds equity in an asset. Renting means paying less per month and investing the difference elsewhere. The question is which pot grows faster: home equity plus appreciation, or your investment portfolio minus rent.

A worked example you can check

Take a ₹60 lakh flat in a tier-1 city. You pay ₹12 lakh down and borrow ₹48 lakh at 8.5% for 20 years. The EMI calculator puts the EMI at about ₹41,700. Renting the same flat costs about ₹25,000 a month. So renting frees up roughly ₹16,700 a month (the gap widens every year as rent rises ~7% while the EMI stays fixed, but stay with the simple case).

Invest that ₹16,700 monthly at 10% (a reasonable long-run equity assumption, not a promise) and after 20 years you hold about ₹1.28 crore. The house, meanwhile, cost you roughly ₹22 lakh in interest over 20 years, plus maintenance, property tax, and stamp duty upfront. For the house route to win, the flat's value after 20 years must beat your invested corpus plus the down payment's opportunity cost. At 5% annual property appreciation the flat reaches about ₹1.59 crore; at 3% it is only ₹1.08 crore. That spread is the whole debate in miniature.

What the math usually says

  • Renting wins when rent is below ~3% of the property's value per year, when you might move cities within a few years, and when property appreciation in your area looks weaker than equity returns.
  • Buying wins when you will stay put for 7+ years, the EMI is under about a third of household income, rent is rising fast in your city, and you value the certainty of owning (a real, non-financial benefit).
  • The hidden decider is discipline. The rent-and-invest plan only works if the difference genuinely gets invested. For many people, the forced saving of an EMI is worth more than the theoretical returns of a plan they will not follow.
Non-financial factors are real. Security of tenure, freedom to renovate, school catchments, and family stability are legitimate reasons to buy even when renting would mathematically come out ahead. Money is a tool here, not the scorekeeper.

A middle path many families take

Buy when the numbers are broadly neutral or better for you, with a comfortable down payment (20%+), an EMI under a third of take-home pay, and a horizon beyond seven years in the same city. Keep renting when any of those fail. Revisit the decision yearly with fresh numbers rather than deciding once under social pressure.

Run your own numbers

  1. Get the EMI for your target property with the EMI calculator.
  2. Subtract your current rent to find the monthly difference.
  3. Project that difference with the SIP calculator over your horizon.
  4. Compare against realistic (not optimistic) local appreciation. Be honest about which number you are assuming.
